## The Problem Nobody Owns by Default

Deploying enterprise AI gets you tools people *can* use. The question that arrives next is easy to leave unowned: when an AI system wants to act — merge code, post updates, read financial systems — what does "ready" have to mean, and who puts the evidence in front of the people who decide?

At Willow Wealth the decision itself sits with the control functions, such as compliance, security and IT. My side is readiness: I run AI tools and connectors through a ratified risk-tier framework that sets what each tier may do across a ~120-person company, keep the approved-tool registry, and put the evidence in front of the people who decide, in an environment where FINRA and SEC obligations make "what ships" a compliance decision as much as a technical one.

## Ready, and Not Yet

A readiness check that has never said "not yet" is a rubber stamp. Two calls on my own work from the same period:

**Kept live, under challenge.** I migrated our org-wide AI-to-observability integration off a deprecated endpoint onto the supported one. A senior engineer asked me to defer it — an incident-opening automation he relied on used an adjacent access path, and he was about to be unreachable. Instead of rolling back, I pulled the platform's own audit trail: his path was untouched by the change. He could check the same trail himself, and signed off shortly after. The rule this left me with: when someone asks you to defer a change that's already live, the answer is evidence about their specific concern, not a reflexive rollback.

**Not yet, on my own work.** A fully-prepared org-wide permissions rollout — payloads staged, pre-deploy checks green — stayed unshipped: deploying it as a single blob could have silently applied the wrong security posture to one cohort of users. "Ready" and "safe to ship" are different claims, and the gap between them was a scoping question nobody had answered yet. I wrote the hold down, with the reasoning, in the decision record.

## What "Good Enough to Ship" Actually Means

Codified, not vibes. The bar an AI deployment has to clear before it goes to the people who approve it:

- **Read-only by construction** — write access is always a separate governance request
- **Human-gated** — no auto-merge, no unreviewed actions on sensitive surfaces
- **Hard exclusions** — nothing that touches money movement, KYC, or PII
- **Provenance** — automated writes carry who/what/why, with a deny list underneath
- **Approval ≠ go-live** — sign-off and activation are separate, deliberate steps
- **A defined rollback or kill path** — before launch, not after
- **Pre-launch success targets** for anything classed as financial-impact

Anything investor-facing routes through compliance review (FINRA 2210) before it ships. As of September 2026 nothing investor-facing had shipped — deliberately; that was the gate working as designed, not a backlog. The same gate applies to my own prototypes.

The bar covers numbers as well as deployments: when the value estimate counted pull requests created rather than merged, the conservative merged-PR basis is what went to leadership — an estimated value ratio of roughly 2 to 1 in a reading taken on 1 September 2026, against roughly 3 to 1 on the created-PR basis.

## Speed Is Part of the Bar

Governance that's slow gets routed around. When a non-technical operations lead filed an intake for a Slack-to-project-tracker automation — the AI itself had flagged her design as needing review — I approved the pilot the same day, on two written privacy conditions: aggregate-level data only, and PII rules apply to every post. It has run under those conditions since, built and operated by its non-technical owner.

The same posture got an operations workflow to a working internal POC in one sitting: a business-operations lead spec'd her services team's manual pre-call preparation process in a 1:1, and about two hours later a pre-call brief generator — read-only, sensitive fields excluded, gaps stated rather than filled — was running against real data. A POC, not a team-wide launch; readiness criteria built in from the first prompt are what made that pace possible. Under the same model, a read-only ERP connector went live for Finance's month-end close — named users, scoped access, written usage rules — and that scoped, read-only service-account pattern became the standard answer whenever another system asks for AI access.

The test of the whole posture is whether the org kept using the tools: weekly active use peaked above 90% of the ~120 seats, with Claude Code on 81.7% of merged pull requests in August 2026.

## Patterns

1. **Evidence from the system beats persuasion.** The audit trail settled a colleague's objection to a live change in minutes — and separately disproved an AI surface's own claim that an integration was blocked (access turned out to be endpoint-scoped, not blocked). When the evidence is checkable by both parties, the argument usually ends.

2. **A hold you can explain is a feature.** Writing down *why* something didn't ship builds more trust than shipping it would have.

3. **Approval is not go-live.** Separating them lets the approvers say yes quickly without anything going live accidentally.

4. **Speed keeps governance alive.** A same-day conditioned approval gets used; a four-week review board gets bypassed.
